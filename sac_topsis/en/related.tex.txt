The optimization of task offloading in vehicular networks has traditionally been approached as a multi-objective trade-off between \acrotip{JCT} and network energy consumption~\cite{liu2022multitask_mec,fang2021mec_iot}. Early works sought to balance these metrics using deterministic methods based on \acrotip{MCDM}. The FOFF framework~\cite{vieira2025foff}, for example, applied the Fuzzy-TOPSIS technique to rank edge servers based on their instantaneous capacities, obtaining significant energy savings without the need for a prior training phase. However, models based exclusively on logical rules or instantaneous heuristic evaluations fail in non-stationary vehicular scenarios, as they cannot anticipate fluctuations in queue dynamics or learn from continuous performance history. To overcome this adaptive rigidity, the community has begun to investigate hybrid approaches that combine quantitative filters with machine learning strategies, which is an active branch in contemporary literature~\cite{uddin2024intelligent}.

With the maturation of learning architectures, \acrotip{DRL} has gained substantial traction due to its innate ability for online adaptation in Markovian decision environments. Within the scope of vehicular allocation, Deep Q-Networks (\acrotip{DQN}) and their advantage variants are commonly applied to selection problems with discrete action spaces~\cite{mnih2015,uddin2025}. In contrast, continuous and high-dimensional configurations require architectures based on the actor-critic paradigm, such as \acrotip{PPO}, Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (\acrotip{DDPG}), and \acrotip{TD3}~\cite{schulman2017ppo,bao2025}. Despite establishing the state of the art in controlled scenarios, current \acrotip{DRL} methods face considerable challenges in the transition to the real world. A widespread structural limitation is the dependence on a strictly fixed-size observation space, as the vast majority of models use zero-padding or truncation to accommodate environments with a variable number of candidates~\cite{miriyala2026dl_offload_review}. This flat vectorization practice forces neural networks to learn spurious artifacts and noise resulting from the breakdown of input geometric symmetry, drastically degrading generalization performance and preventing zero-shot adaptation to traffic density variations.

Maximum entropy off-policy algorithms, such as \acrotip{SAC}, mitigate part of this challenge by offering more robust exploration and superior sampling efficiency compared to conventional on-policy methods. However, even high-capacity models remain widely susceptible to temporal instability and representation collapse when subjected to poorly formulated or uninformative state descriptions~\cite{haarnoja2018sac}.

In an attempt to strengthen environment representation, recent works have coupled \acrotip{MCDM} preprocessing with \acrotip{DRL} decisions but almost invariably treat multi-criteria filtering as a post-processing element designed to rank finite sets of actions already issued by the agent. \acrotip{SAC}-\acrotip{TOPSIS} differs substantially from these approaches as it consolidates the \acrotip{TOPSIS} technique internally within the observation edge, acting strictly as a state engineering module. The output computed by this module does not dictate the final decision but condenses local characteristics into a strictly compact continuous state encoding (6D vector) that is perfectly scale-invariant, eliminating the dependence on the notorious zero-padding and extinguishing any assumption of rigid topology. Fundamentally, the compression process established by \acrotip{TOPSIS} is agnostic to the control algorithm, as our experimental evaluation attests that pairing this structured space with \acrotip{SAC}, \acrotip{TD3}, and \acrotip{PPO} algorithms produces direct and consistent gains over their traditional variants fed with raw observations. This validates the central thesis that the compositional state engineering developed via \acrotip{TOPSIS}, rather than the particular \acrotip{DRL} policy structure, acts as the primary driver for the spatial generalization capability exhibited by the intelligent agent.

Maximum entropy off-policy algorithms, such as Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), mitigate part of this challenge by offering more robust exploration and superior sampling efficiency compared to conventional on-policy methods. However, even high-capacity models remain widely susceptible to temporal instability and representation collapse when subjected to poorly formulated or uninformative state descriptions~\cite{haarnoja2018sac}.

In an attempt to strengthen environment representation, recent works have coupled Multi-Criteria Decision Making (MCDM) preprocessing with Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) decisions but almost invariably treat multi-criteria filtering as a post-processing element designed to rank finite sets of actions already issued by the agent. The SAC-TOPSIS architecture proposed in this intermediate stage differs substantially from these approaches as it consolidates the Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) internally within the observation edge, acting strictly as a state engineering module. This module evaluates the edge server (VEC) and neighboring vehicles based on estimated job completion time (JCT), energy consumption, and distance to the computing node to identify the best offloading candidate. The computed output does not dictate the final decision but condenses these characteristics into a strictly compact continuous state encoding configured as a perfectly scale-invariant 6D vector, eliminating the dependence on zero-padding and extinguishing any assumption of rigid topology. Fundamentally, the compression process established by TOPSIS is agnostic to the control algorithm. Our experimental evaluation compared five DRL families (SAC, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3), Deep Q-Network (DQN), and Advantage Actor-Critic (A2C)) and seven reference heuristics, attesting that pairing this structured space produces direct and consistent gains over traditional variants fed with raw observations. This validates the central thesis that the compositional state engineering developed via TOPSIS acts as the primary driver for the spatial generalization capability exhibited by the intelligent agent, as it outperforms heuristics and pure DRL models by reducing the impact of candidate count variability.
